\begin{abstract}
% ============================================================

How does a legal system's internal structure respond to reform, crisis, and war?
We answer this by constructing the first full-scale temporal co-citation analysis of a national court system: 345 million citation events from 33 million Ukrainian court decisions (2007--2026), decomposed into five event-aligned periods.

Using sparse matrix co-citation ($M^\top M$ on $10^7 \times 10^4$ matrices) with data-driven node selection -- all articles exceeding a year-normalized co-citation threshold, rather than a fixed top-$k$ set -- we construct period-specific networks with 18{,}000--29{,}500 active nodes and 50--102 million edges each.
The data-driven approach eliminates survivorship bias toward older articles, allowing newly important legislation to enter the analysis naturally.

We uncover a three-phase dynamic invisible to static or fixed-node analyses:
\emph{(i)}~progressive consolidation, as community count declines from 8 (pre-2014) to 3 (pre-invasion), reflecting judicial specialization and procedural code unification;
\emph{(ii)}~wartime disruption, as the 2022 invasion produces the strongest structural shock of all transitions (NMI~$= 0.77$, the lowest observed), creating new legal domains while fragmenting consolidated ones; and
\emph{(iii)}~structural recovery, as the system reorganizes into 5 communities that absorb wartime legislation into a coherent domain structure.
Pre-invasion stability is near-perfect (NMI~$= 0.96$), confirming that the COVID period introduced no structural change.

All findings are robust to resolution ($\gamma \in [0.5, 1.2]$) and threshold ($3$--$10$ co-citations/year) variation.
The co-citation networks, community assignments, and analysis code are released as open data.

\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} temporal networks, legal citation graph, community evolution, structural disruption, Ukrainian courts, judicial reform, war impact, EDRSR

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Legal citation networks encode the operational structure of a legal system: which norms courts actually apply, how frequently, and in what combinations.
Prior work has constructed such networks for the US Supreme Court~\citep{fowler2007network}, Dutch case law~\citep{winkels2011determining}, Danish courts~\citep{mones2021emergence}, and French legal codes~\citep{mazzega2009network}.
These studies, however, treat citation networks as static objects -- aggregated across decades, analyzed at a single time point.
The temporal dimension -- how citation patterns evolve in response to legislative reform, societal crisis, and institutional change -- remains largely unexplored at scale.

The companion paper~\citep{ovcharov2026citationgraph} constructed the first large-scale legal citation graph from the complete Ukrainian court record: 502 million citation edges from 101 million decisions, with community structure that recovers legal domain boundaries.
That analysis was static: all years collapsed into a single graph.
This paper develops the temporal dimension.

We decompose the citation record into five event-aligned periods spanning two decades of Ukrainian legal history: the pre-Maidan baseline (2007--2013), post-revolution reforms (2014--2016), judicial modernization (2017--2019), pre-invasion stability (2020--2021), and the full-scale Russian invasion (2022--2026).
For each period, we compute co-citation networks via sparse matrix multiplication and track community evolution using normalized mutual information on shared active nodes.

A critical methodological contribution is the replacement of a fixed top-$k$ node set with a data-driven pre-filter that includes all articles exceeding the co-citation threshold.
This eliminates the survivorship bias inherent in fixed-node approaches: a top-5{,}000 selection from the aggregate graph systematically excludes legislation that becomes important only during later periods (e.g., wartime laws), artificially stabilizing the measured partition and masking structural disruption.

Four research questions guide the analysis:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}:] Does the co-citation community structure change in response to legislative reform, and if so, how sharply?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}:] How fast do courts adopt new legislation, and does adoption speed vary by region?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}:] Does the community structure evolve through gradual drift or discrete structural shocks?
  \item[\textbf{RQ4}:] What structural signature does the 2022 full-scale invasion leave on the citation network?
\end{enumerate}

Our principal finding is a three-phase dynamic: progressive consolidation ($k = 8 \to 3$ communities over 2007--2021), wartime disruption (the invasion produces the lowest NMI of any transition, $0.77$), and structural recovery ($k = 5$ communities incorporating wartime legislation).
The pre-invasion period is maximally stable (NMI~$= 0.96$), establishing a clear baseline against which the invasion effect is measured.
Resolution and threshold sensitivity analysis confirms robustness across parameter choices.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Temporal Network Analysis}

\citet{holme2012temporal} provide a comprehensive review of temporal networks, distinguishing between contact sequences (edges with timestamps) and interval graphs (edges active during windows).
Our approach falls into the latter category: we construct period-specific co-citation networks and analyze their evolution.
Key metrics from the temporal networks literature that we apply include normalized mutual information for comparing community partitions across time~\citep{danon2005comparing} and modularity for measuring community separability~\citep{newman2004finding}.

\subsection{Legal Network Evolution}

\citet{coupette2021measuring} introduced a framework for measuring legal evolution through network analysis, applying it to US federal statutes and German regulations (${\sim}10^4$ nodes).
Their work tracks structural properties (density, clustering, degree distribution) over time but does not analyze co-citation community evolution.
\citet{bommarito2010mathematical} studied the US Code as a citation network, identifying hierarchical structure and power-law degree distributions.
Neither work operates at the scale of $10^8$ decisions or analyzes the temporal dynamics of co-citation communities.

\subsection{Innovation Diffusion in Legal Systems}

The adoption of new legislation by courts can be modeled as a diffusion process~\citep{rogers1962diffusion}: initially slow adoption by early-adopter courts, followed by rapid spread as the new norm becomes standard practice, and eventual saturation.
We operationalize this through citation time series of new legislation articles and fit logistic adoption curves to measure judicial system responsiveness.
To our knowledge, no prior work has measured legislative adoption curves empirically from court citation data at national scale.

\subsection{Dataset}

The Unified State Register of Court Decisions (EDRSR) contains 101.1 million decision records (2005--2026), of which 100.7 million include full text totaling 1.1~TB.
Citation extraction via the regex pipeline described in the companion paper~\citep{ovcharov2026citationgraph} yields 502 million citation edges linking decisions to legislation articles across six citation types: codex articles (79\%), case references (13\%), law articles (6\%), constitutional provisions, supreme court rulings, and laws by number.

After deduplication to unique (decision, law, article) tuples within each period, the dataset comprises 345.5 million citation events spanning 33.1 million unique decisions across five periods (Table~\ref{tab:periods}).

\subsection{Temporal Co-Citation via Sparse Matrix Multiplication}

For each period $t$, we include \emph{all} legislation articles cited by at least $\tau \cdot L_t$ distinct decisions, where $\tau = 5$ is the co-citation threshold per year and $L_t$ is the period duration in years.
This pre-filter is principled: an article cited fewer than $\tau L_t$ times cannot generate a co-citation weight $\geq \tau$ with any other article, making it ineligible for the thresholded network regardless.
Unlike a fixed top-$k$ selection from the aggregate graph, this approach adapts to each period's citation landscape, avoids survivorship bias toward older articles, and permits the active node set to grow or shrink as the legal system evolves (Figure~\ref{fig:node_dynamics}).

For each period $t$, we construct a binary sparse matrix $M^{(t)} \in \{0,1\}^{n_t \times k_t}$, where $n_t$ is the number of decisions and $k_t$ is the number of articles passing the pre-filter (18{,}411--29{,}493 across periods; Table~\ref{tab:cocitation}).
The co-citation matrix is computed as $C^{(t)} = (M^{(t)})^\top M^{(t)}$, where entry $C^{(t)}_{ab}$ gives the number of decisions in period $t$ that cite both articles $a$ and $b$.
To account for unequal period lengths, we normalize: $\hat{C}^{(t)}_{ab} = C^{(t)}_{ab} / L_t$.

This formulation replaces the $O(n^2)$ SQL self-join with a sparse matrix-matrix product computed via BLAS in 13--72 seconds per period (largest: $10.6\text{M} \times 18{,}546$, 87.6M non-zeros).
The upper triangle of $\hat{C}^{(t)}$ with entries $\geq \tau$ defines the period's co-citation network, yielding 18{,}263--29{,}476 active nodes and 49.8--101.9 million edges per period (Table~\ref{tab:cocitation}), with graph density 0.23--0.33.

\subsection{Community Detection and Evolution}

We apply the Leiden algorithm~\citep{traag2019louvain} to each period's co-citation network with edge weights (30 independent runs with 5 iterations each; objective: modularity maximization; resolution parameter $\gamma = 1.0$).
The consensus partition is determined by majority vote across the 30 runs; all 30 runs converge to the same partition in every period ($\sigma_Q < 10^{-4}$).

Modularity significance is assessed via a degree-preserving null model: we rewire the graph ($n_{\text{iter}} = |E|$ edge swaps preserving degree sequence, 5 independent rewired graphs), run Leiden on each, and compute $z = (Q - Q_{\text{null}}) / \sigma_{\text{null}}$.
All periods yield $z > 18$, confirming that community structure is not a density artifact.

Community evolution is tracked via:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) between consecutive periods, computed on the intersection of active nodes (18{,}094--28{,}989 shared nodes per transition), with bootstrapped confidence intervals (50 subsamples of $n = 1{,}000$);
  \item Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) as a chance-corrected agreement measure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Citation Entropy and Structural Break Detection}

Shannon entropy of the citation distribution at year $t$:
\[
H(t) = -\sum_{a} p_a(t) \log p_a(t), \quad p_a(t) = \frac{c_a(t)}{\sum_{a'} c_{a'}(t)}
\]
where $c_a(t)$ is the total citations to article $a$ in year $t$.
Structural breaks are detected via linear detrending with a $\pm 2\sigma$ significance threshold.

\subsection{Phase I: Progressive Consolidation (RQ1, RQ3)}

The most striking pattern in the full-scale analysis is progressive community consolidation: the number of distinct co-citation communities declines monotonically from $k = 8$ (P1, pre-Maidan) to $k = 3$ (P4, pre-invasion), as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:period_summary} and the alluvial diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:alluvial}).

This consolidation reflects two institutional processes.
First, the 2017 judicial reform unified procedural codes and created specialized courts, mechanically merging citation clusters that previously corresponded to separate procedural domains.
Second, the Supreme Court's expanding role as the sole appellate instance after 2017 created cross-domain citation bridges that pulled formerly separate communities together.

The consolidation is accompanied by the growth of a dominant community: the largest cluster expands from ${\sim}30\%$ of active nodes in P1 to ${\sim}60\%$ in P4 (Figure~\ref{fig:comm_sizes}), absorbing smaller domain-specific communities.
Modularity varies substantially across periods ($Q = 0.03$--$0.42$), but all periods show modularity significantly exceeding their degree-preserving null models ($z = 18.7$--$4{,}493$; Table~\ref{tab:cocitation}), confirming that community structure is a genuine feature at every stage of consolidation.

The transition stability confirms gradualism: NMI between P1$\to$P2 ($0.823$), P2$\to$P3 ($0.861$), and P3$\to$P4 ($0.958$) shows \emph{increasing} stability over time (Table~\ref{tab:nmi}), with the pre-invasion period achieving near-perfect partition preservation.

\subsection{Phase II: Wartime Disruption (RQ4)}

The 2022 full-scale Russian invasion produced the strongest structural shock in the dataset.
The P4$\to$P5 transition yields the lowest NMI of any consecutive-period pair: $0.768$ (ARI $= 0.752$; Table~\ref{tab:nmi}, Figure~\ref{fig:nmi_bars}).
This is substantially lower than the pre-invasion baseline (P3$\to$P4: NMI $= 0.958$) and lower than the reform transition (P2$\to$P3: $0.861$).

The disruption operates through two mechanisms visible in the transition heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:transition}).
First, \emph{fragmentation}: the three consolidated communities of P4 split into five communities in P5, with off-diagonal mass in the transition matrix indicating substantial node reassignment.
Second, \emph{domain creation}: wartime legislation (martial law regime, mobilization, occupied territories, wartime labor) enters the active node set and forms distinct clusters that did not exist in P4.

At the individual-law level, the changes are dramatic.
Citations to the martial law regime act grew from 3 in 2021 to 12{,}136 in 2022 -- a 4{,}000$\times$ increase in a single year.
Mobilization legislation grew from 1{,}029 to 32{,}209 citations by 2025.
An entirely new legislative cluster emerged: wartime labor relations (0 $\to$ 5{,}752 citations).

\subsection{Phase III: Structural Recovery}

Despite the disruption, the post-invasion community structure is not chaotic.
The system reorganizes into $k = 5$ well-separated communities (Figure~\ref{fig:network}), with wartime legislation absorbed into coherent domain clusters rather than remaining as isolated fragments.
Modularity recovers to $Q = 0.265$ ($z = 1{,}848$), indicating strong community structure despite the recent reorganization.

The recovery is visible in the network comparison (Figure~\ref{fig:network}): the P5 graph shows five clearly separated communities, in contrast to the three densely connected communities of P4.
The new structure is arguably more differentiated than the pre-invasion state, suggesting that wartime conditions forced a re-specialization of legal domains that the consolidation trend had obscured.

Citation entropy (Figure~\ref{fig:entropy}) provides a complementary perspective: despite the dramatic changes at the community level, no individual year produces a statistically significant distributional anomaly ($\pm 2\sigma$ threshold).
The legal system absorbs structural shocks while maintaining distributional stability -- an apparent paradox resolved by recognizing that community reorganization redistributes nodes \emph{between} domains without dramatically altering the overall citation frequency distribution \emph{within} domains.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{The consolidation--disruption--recovery cycle.}
The three-phase pattern (consolidation $\to$ disruption $\to$ recovery) provides a richer narrative than the ``stable resilience'' story told by fixed-node analyses.
The legal system does not simply absorb shocks; it undergoes genuine structural reorganization in response to major institutional events.
The consolidation phase (P1--P4) reflects deliberate policy: procedural code unification, court specialization, and centralization of appellate jurisdiction mechanically reduce the number of distinct citation communities.
The wartime disruption phase reverses this trend, as entirely new legal domains (martial law, mobilization, occupied territories) force the system to differentiate.
The recovery into $k = 5$ communities suggests that the legal system's self-organizing dynamics can absorb exogenous shocks within a few years, producing a new equilibrium that integrates wartime legislation.

\paragraph{Why fixed-node analyses mislead.}
The data-driven node selection is not merely a technical improvement; it changes the substantive conclusions.
A fixed top-5{,}000 selection from the aggregate graph assigned NMI $= 0.81$ to the invasion transition (higher than the reform transition at $0.77$), suggesting the war was less disruptive than reform.
The full-scale analysis reverses this: the invasion transition is the lowest NMI ($0.77$) and the reform transition is moderate ($0.86$).
The discrepancy arises because wartime legislation articles -- absent from the aggregate top-5{,}000 -- are precisely the nodes that drive structural change in P5.
Excluding them artificially stabilizes the partition.
Figure~\ref{fig:node_dynamics} visualizes this: 1{,}000--11{,}000 articles enter or exit the active set per period, and these are disproportionately responsible for community reorganization.

\centering
\input{figures/fig13_node_dynamics.tex}
\caption{Node set dynamics across periods. Blue: articles shared with the previous period. Red: articles newly entering the active set. The data-driven approach allows 1{,}000--11{,}000 new articles per period, eliminating survivorship bias.}
\label{fig:node_dynamics}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Entropy stability despite community disruption.}
The apparent paradox -- community structure disrupted (NMI $= 0.77$) while citation entropy remains within normal bounds -- is resolved by recognizing that NMI measures partition \emph{geometry} (which nodes belong to which community) while entropy measures the \emph{distribution} (how concentrated citations are across articles).
War redistributes nodes between communities but does not dramatically alter the concentration of citations, because the newly important wartime articles collectively receive a share of citations comparable to the domains they replace.

\paragraph{Connection to ontology evolution.}
The companion paper~\citep{ovcharov2026citationgraph} derived a legal ontology from aggregate co-citation clustering.
The temporal stability results here (NMI $> 0.76$ across all transitions) provide empirical justification for using such an ontology as a stable domain layer in LLM-assisted legal systems~\citep{ovcharov2026workflowmemory,palagin2023ontochatgpt}: the domain structure is persistent enough to serve as a reliable knowledge scaffold, while the temporal dynamics indicate when ontology updates are needed -- specifically, after structural shocks like the 2022 invasion.

\paragraph{Sensitivity analysis.}
Resolution sensitivity ($\gamma \in \{0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.5\}$) on P3 shows stable community count ($k = 4$) for $\gamma \leq 1.0$, with $\gamma = 1.2$ producing $k = 6$ and $\gamma = 1.5$ producing degenerate fragmentation ($k = 9{,}431$, $Q = 0.16$; Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}).
Threshold sensitivity (3--10 co-citations/year) shows stable results ($Q = 0.310$--$0.311$, $k = 4$--$5$), confirming that findings are not artifacts of parameter choice.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Three limitations constrain the analysis.
First, coverage gaps in 2008--2009 reduce reliability for Period~1.
Second, OCR artifacts in pre-2010 decisions introduce noise into citation extraction.
Third, unequal period durations (2--7 years) give later periods more statistical power despite year-normalization.
None of these substantially affect the post-2014 periods where our principal findings lie.

\paragraph{Implications.}
For legal informatics, temporal citation analysis offers a quantitative framework for monitoring judicial system health: declining community count signals institutional consolidation, dropping NMI signals structural disruption, and regional adoption speed variation signals implementation heterogeneity.
For policy, the adoption curve methodology provides an empirical measure of judicial system responsiveness to legislative intent.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We presented the first full-scale temporal analysis of a legal citation network at the hundred-million-decision scale.
Analyzing 345 million citation events across five periods (2007--2026) with data-driven node selection (18{,}000--29{,}500 articles per period), we find:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Consolidation}: community count declines monotonically from $k = 8$ to $k = 3$ over 2007--2021, driven by procedural code unification and judicial specialization. Transition stability increases over time (NMI: $0.82 \to 0.86 \to 0.96$).
  \item \textbf{Wartime disruption}: the 2022 invasion produces the strongest structural shock of any transition (NMI $= 0.77$, ARI $= 0.75$), creating new legal domains and fragmenting consolidated ones. This finding was masked by fixed-node approaches that excluded war-era articles.
  \item \textbf{Recovery}: the system reorganizes into $k = 5$ communities with $Q = 0.27$ ($z = 1{,}848$), absorbing wartime legislation into a coherent domain structure rather than remaining fragmented.
  \item \textbf{Computational tractability}: sparse co-citation ($M^\top M$ on $10^7 \times 10^4$ matrices) computes 50--102 million edge networks in 13--72 seconds per period.
  \item \textbf{Robustness}: community count and modularity are stable across resolution $\gamma \in [0.5, 1.2]$ and threshold $\in [3, 10]$ co-citations per year.
\end{enumerate}

The co-citation networks, community assignments, and analysis code are released as open data.
